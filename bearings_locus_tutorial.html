Part A — Three-figure Bearings

1 What is a Bearing?

A bearing is a way of describing a direction using an angle measured from North. Bearings are used in navigation, surveying, aviation and map work.

Three rules of bearings
  • Measured from North, turning clockwise.
  • Always written with three figures — 047°, not 47°.
  • Between 000° and 360° inclusive.

North line

Every point has its own North line, drawn straight up the page. A bearing is always measured from the North line at the point you are looking from.

“Of B from A” — the bearing is measured at A (the “from” point) and points towards B. Swapping A and B gives a different bearing!

2 Measuring & Drawing Bearings

To find the bearing of one point from another on a diagram, you use a protractor.

Method
  1. Draw a North line at the point you are measuring from.
  2. Place the protractor’s centre on that point.
  3. Line up the 0° mark with the North line (not the East–West line).
  4. Read the scale going clockwise to the line joining the two points.
  5. Write the answer as three figures.
Common mistakes: measuring anticlockwise · using the wrong protractor scale (inner instead of outer) · forgetting the leading zero (47° instead of 047°) · measuring from the wrong North line.

3 Back Bearings (Reciprocal Bearings)

If you know the bearing of B from A, the bearing of A from B is called the back bearing (or reciprocal bearing).

Rule: Add 180° if the bearing is less than 180°; subtract 180° if it is 180° or more.

060°  →  060 + 180 = 240°    300°  →  300 − 180 = 120°

4 Bearings with Scale Drawing & Trigonometry

Bearings questions often give distances as well as directions. You can solve them by accurate scale drawing or by trigonometry.

Method A — Scale drawing

Steps
  1. Choose a scale, e.g. 1 cm : 5 km.
  2. Draw a North line at the start point and mark the first bearing with a protractor.
  3. Measure the given distance along it using your scale.
  4. Draw a North line at the new point and mark the next bearing.
  5. Join the final points; measure the required length/angle and convert back using the scale.

Method B — Trigonometry

Bearings create triangles. Turn the bearings into interior angles, then use:

  • Pythagoras’ theorem when the angle between the two paths is 90°.
  • Cosine rule a² = b² + c² − 2bc·cos A for the side opposite a known angle.
  • Sine rule a / sin A = b / sin B for finding a missing angle or side.
Worked example. A ship sails 7 km from O on a bearing of 030° to P, then another ship leaves O and sails 10 km on a bearing of 100° to Q. Find PQ.

Angle POQ = 100° − 30° = 70°. Using the cosine rule:
PQ² = 7² + 10² − 2(7)(10)cos 70° = 49 + 100 − 47.9 = 101.1
PQ ≈ 10.1 km

Part B — Locus

5 What is a Locus?

A locus (plural loci) is the set of all points that satisfy a given condition — or, in other words, the path traced by a moving point obeying a rule.

Think of it like this: a goat tied to a post by a rope walks wherever the rope allows. The path its boundary traces is a locus — in this case a circle.

There are four standard two-dimensional loci you must know. Each one has a simple name and a simple construction.

6 The Four Loci — Animated

6.1 · Fixed distance from a point → a circle

The locus of points a distance r from a fixed point A is the circle with centre A and radius r.

🎯 Locus lab 1 — the circle

A moving point stays exactly r from A → it traces a circle. In 3-D, a ball can move anywhere on the surface of a sphere.

6.2 · Fixed distance from a straight line → two parallel lines

The locus of points a distance d from a line l is two lines parallel to l, one on each side, each distance d away.

📏 Locus lab 2 — parallel lines

Both red lines form the locus — a point above or below l is allowed. In 3-D this becomes the surface of a cylinder.

6.3 · Equidistant from two points → perpendicular bisector

The locus of points the same distance from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB.

⚖️ Locus lab 3 — perpendicular bisector

Construction: draw arcs of equal radius from A and B; the line through the two crossing points is the locus.

6.4 · Equidistant from two intersecting lines → angle bisectors

The locus of points the same distance from two lines that cross is the pair of angle bisectors of the angles between them.

✂️ Locus lab 4 — angle bisectors

The point is the same perpendicular distance from both lines — that is exactly what “equidistant from two lines” means.

7 Intersecting Loci

To locate a point that must satisfy two conditions at once, draw each locus separately. The point(s) where the loci intersect satisfy both conditions.

Method of intersecting loci
  1. Translate each condition into a locus (circle, parallel lines, bisector…).
  2. Draw the first locus accurately with compasses and ruler.
  3. Draw the second locus on the same diagram.
  4. Mark the intersection point(s) — these are the required positions.
  5. If the question says “shade the region”, shade the area that satisfies all the conditions.

Worked example — intersecting loci

A treasure is buried 4 m from a tree T and 6 m from a rock R, where T and R are 9 m apart.

  • Locus 1: circle, centre T, radius 4 m.
  • Locus 2: circle, centre R, radius 6 m.
  • The circles cross at two points, so there are two possible treasure sites. You would need a third condition to choose between them.
Region / shading questions. When you see words like “within”, “less than”, “closer to”, the answer is a shaded region rather than a single line. Example: “within 3 cm of A but more than 2 cm from line l” means inside the circle of radius 3 cm centred on A, and outside the two lines parallel to l at distance 2 cm.
