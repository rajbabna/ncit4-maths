Part 1

Single Events

Probability tells us how likely something is to happen. Every probability is a number between 0 and 1 — written as a fraction or decimal, never as a ratio.

The Core Formula
Probability of an event P(event) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total possible outcomes

The result must always be written as a fraction (e.g. 1/6) or a decimal (e.g. 0.167). Writing a ratio like 1:6 is incorrect in probability.

0Impossible
0.25Unlikely
0.5Even chance
0.75Likely
1Certain
Important rules to remember:
  • P(event) is always between 0 and 1 inclusive.
  • P(event does NOT happen) = 1 − P(event happens)
  • The probabilities of all possible outcomes always add up to 1.
A Worked Example — Fair Six-Sided Die

A fair die is rolled once. Find: (i) P(rolling a 4)   (ii) P(rolling an even number)   (iii) P(rolling a number greater than 4)

i
Favourable outcomes for rolling a 4: just {4} → 1 outcome.
Total outcomes: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} → 6 outcomes.
P(4) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167
ii
Favourable outcomes (even): {2, 4, 6} → 3 outcomes.
P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5
iii
Favourable outcomes (greater than 4): {5, 6} → 2 outcomes.
P(>4) = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333
B Worked Example — Bag of Marbles

A bag contains 5 red, 3 blue, and 2 green marbles. One marble is drawn at random. Find P(blue) and P(not green).

1
Total marbles: 5 + 3 + 2 = 10
2
P(blue) = 3/10 = 0.3
3
P(green) = 2/10 = 0.2, so P(not green) = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8
Part 2

Possibility Diagrams

When two events happen together (like rolling two dice), we can map every possible outcome on a grid. Each point on the grid represents one combined outcome.

How to read a possibility diagram
  • The x-axis shows the outcomes of one event (e.g. Die 1).
  • The y-axis shows the outcomes of the second event (e.g. Die 2).
  • Each point (dot) on the grid is one possible combined outcome.
  • Count the dots that satisfy your condition, then divide by the total number of dots.
C Worked Example — Two Dice, Sum = 7

Two fair dice are rolled. Use the possibility diagram to find P(sum = 7).

Green dots = outcomes where sum is 7. Grey dots = all other outcomes.

Result: Green dots (sum = 7): (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) → 6 outcomes
Total outcomes: 36  →  P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167
How to set up a possibility diagram:
  1. Label the x-axis with the outcomes of the first event.
  2. Label the y-axis with the outcomes of the second event.
  3. Mark a dot at every crossing point (every combination).
  4. Identify and count the dots that match your condition.
  5. Write P = (matching dots) ÷ (total dots) as a fraction or decimal.
Part 3

Tree Diagrams

A tree diagram branches out every possible outcome step by step. Probabilities sit on the branches; outcomes are written at the tips. It is especially useful when events happen one after another.

Key rules for tree diagrams
Multiply along branches
To find the probability of a sequence of outcomes, multiply the probabilities on each branch.
Add separate routes
If an event can happen via more than one path, add the probabilities of each path.
Combined event rule P(A then B) = P(A) × P(B)
D Worked Example — Flipping a Coin Twice

A fair coin is flipped twice. Draw a tree diagram and find: (i) P(HH)   (ii) P(exactly one head)

i
P(HH) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 0.25
ii
Exactly one head can happen two ways: HT or TH.
P(HT) + P(TH) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 2/4 = 1/2 = 0.5
E Worked Example — Coloured Balls Without Replacement

A bag has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find: (i) P(both red)   (ii) P(one of each colour)

i
P(both red) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10 = 0.3
ii
One of each: RB or BR.
P = 6/20 + 6/20 = 12/20 = 3/5 = 0.6
Note: Without replacement means the total changes for the second draw. With 3R+2B, after drawing Red, only 4 balls remain (2R+2B). After drawing Blue, 4 remain (3R+1B).
